This homework walks through the main parts of a modern imitation learning pipeline. I teleoperate a simulated **SO-101** arm to record expert demonstrations, train a policy to imitate the expert actions, and then push the success rate up during evaluation. The task is to pick up a red cube, move it around a wall-shaped obstacle, and drop it into a bin.

The post has four parts: how the data is collected and what the policy looks like, the first two exercises (plain behavior cloning, then DAgger), a closer look at the loss function, and the multi-cube, goal-conditioned task of Exercise 3. I train with a plain **MSE loss**, so the policy predicts the *mean* action, not a *distribution* over actions. That choice explains both why this setup works here and where it will stop working.

## Collecting Expert Data

The first step is recording expert demonstrations by teleoperating the arm with the keyboard. At first this took me a lot of time, because the default camera angle was pretty weird and it was hard to judge depth. After I changed the camera angle, teleoperating the arm became much easier.

Each recorded step stores the simulator state. The actions are then computed as differences between consecutive states, $a_t = s_{t+1} - s_t$. I use end-effector control, so an action is the change of the end-effector position plus a gripper command.

## Policy Architecture

The policy is a simple MLP action-chunking policy:

- **Input (12-dim state):** end-effector position (3), gripper angle (1), cube position plus the first two quaternion components (5), and obstacle position (3). Every dimension is normalized with the mean and std of the training data.
- **Network:** fully connected layers of width 256 with ReLU (5 hidden layers for Exercise 1, 6 for Exercise 2), followed by a linear output layer.
- **Output:** an action chunk of the next 16 steps. Each step is 4-dim: the change of the end-effector position $(dx, dy, dz)$ and the gripper command. At evaluation time the robot executes the whole chunk before predicting the next one.
- **Loss:** MSE between the predicted and the recorded action chunk.
- **Training:** AdamW (lr $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine learning-rate schedule, 200 epochs, batch size 256. For every configuration I train 5 models with seeds 0 to 4.

Predicting a **chunk** of 16 actions instead of a single action is a cheap but effective trick. The policy commits to a short motion segment, so small per-step errors have fewer chances to pile up, and the motion is smoother than re-deciding at every step.

### Why Do the Seeds Differ So Much?

The spread between seeds is huge, from 36% to 88% with the same data and the same hyperparameters. At first I thought the main reason was the train/validation split: the seed decides which samples go into the validation set, and with DAgger data in the mix it matters a lot whether the few DAgger corrections end up in training or get "wasted" in validation.

To be more precise, the seed changes three things at once: the initial weights, the order of the mini-batches, and which 10% of the samples go to the validation set. The split is not the only source of the variance. Even when I trained on the teleop data only, three seeds gave 61%, 73% and 67%. Two changes came out of this:

- **Validation uses teleop data only.** When DAgger data is present, the validation set is sampled only from the original teleop episodes, and all DAgger samples are always used for training. This way no DAgger correction is wasted in the validation set.
- **Model selection by success rate, not by validation loss.** The validation loss does not track the success rate in simulation, so the best-validation checkpoint of a seed is not necessarily a good policy. Instead I evaluate all 5 seeds with the official evaluation (100 episodes, seed 42) and submit the one with the highest success rate.

The second point deserves an explanation. The validation loss measures how close the predicted actions are to the expert's actions, **on states taken from the expert's own trajectories**. The success rate measures what happens when the policy runs **closed-loop**: every small error moves the robot into a slightly different state, the next prediction is made from that state, and errors compound. This mismatch between the training distribution and the states the policy visits is called covariate shift, and it is exactly the problem DAgger is designed to address. A low open-loop MSE says little about whether the policy recovers from its own mistakes.

## A Closer Look at the Loss: MSE Predicts the Mean

One thing I want to highlight is that this whole homework uses only an **MSE loss**:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s, a) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \left\| a - f_\theta(s) \right\|^2 \right]
$$

For a fixed state $s$, the prediction that minimizes this loss is the **conditional mean** of the expert's actions:

$$
f^*(s) = \mathbb{E}[\, a \mid s \,]
$$

You can see this by expanding the loss for a single state: $\mathbb{E}\|a - c\|^2 = \|c - \mathbb{E}[a]\|^2 + \mathrm{Var}(a)$, and the variance term does not depend on the prediction $c$, so the best choice is $c = \mathbb{E}[a]$. Equivalently, MSE is maximum likelihood under a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(f_\theta(s), \sigma^2 I)$ with fixed variance. So an MSE policy implicitly assumes that, for every state, the expert's actions form **one unimodal blob**, and it outputs the center of that blob. It does not learn the **distribution** of actions, so it cannot represent the fact that there may be several equally good ways to act.

### Why That Is a Problem in General

Imagine the expert reaches a wall and goes around it on the left in half of the demos and on the right in the other half. The conditional mean of "go left" and "go right" is "go straight", which drives the arm right into the wall. The averaged action is not just suboptimal; it may not be a valid action at all. This is the **mode averaging** problem, and it is the main weakness of MSE behavior cloning whenever the demonstrations are **multimodal**.

### Why MSE Works in This Task

In this homework, MSE was good enough to reach full points, for a few reasons:

- **The obstacle position is in the input.** In each zone there is basically one correct route, and the network can tell the zones apart from the obstacle position. The multimodality across zones (left detour in the center zone, straight path in the minus zone) is resolved by conditioning, so for a given state the expert's action is close to unimodal.
- **A single, consistent expert.** All demos come from me, with one strategy per zone. The plot of the training data shows how tightly the center-zone teleop demos follow the same detour.
- **Action chunking.** Predicting 16 steps at once makes the policy commit to a whole motion segment, so it is less likely to flip between strategies from one step to the next.
- **Averaging can even help.** Human teleoperation is noisy (small overshoots, pauses, jitter). Predicting the mean smooths that noise out, which is a useful side effect when the noise is just noise and not a different strategy.

Even here there are hints of the limit. In the plus zone, some of my DAgger demos take a wide loop to the left while others go almost straight up. When the data contains two styles like this from similar states, an MSE policy can only output something in between them. That is fine as long as "in between" is still collision-free, but there is no guarantee that it is.

## Exercise 3: A Multi-Cube, Goal-Conditioned Policy

The last exercise is the hardest one. There are now **three cubes** (red, green and blue) and a bin, and both the cube positions and the bin position are randomized at the start of every episode. The policy is told which color to pick through a one-hot goal vector, and it has to put that cube into the bin. The README warns that a success rate approaching 50% is already very strong.

### Breaking Down the Problem

As in Exercise 2, I started by listing what the scene can actually look like instead of recording right away. The randomization turns out to be much smaller than it sounds. There are only four slots along the $y$ axis ($y = 0.35, 0.45, 0.55, 0.70$), and the three cubes plus the bin always fill all four of them. Once the target cube sits in one slot, the bin can only be in one of the other three. That gives $4 \times 3 = 12$ combinations in total:

<img
  src="/images/imitation-learning-practice/multitask-layouts.png"
  alt="Top-down view of the 12 combinations of target slot and bin slot in the multicube scene. In 6 of them the target and the bin are neighbours, in 4 the arm passes over one other cube, and in 2 it passes over two other cubes."
  width={720}
  height={643}
/>

The combinations are not equally hard. In 6 of them the target and the bin are neighbours, in 4 the arm has to carry the cube over one other cube, and in 2 it passes over two.

The second observation was about the colors. There is no vision in this task, since the policy only sees positions, so red, green and blue are just three labels for three positions. The robot does exactly the same thing whichever color is the target: pick up the cube at the target position and drop it into the bin. If I feed all three cube positions plus the one-hot vector into the network, the network first has to learn to read the one-hot and pick the matching position before it can learn anything about grasping. That is an extra problem it would have to solve from a small dataset.

So I solve that part myself, before the network. The one-hot vector simply selects the target position:

$$
p_\text{target} = g_\text{red}\, p_\text{red} + g_\text{green}\, p_\text{green} + g_\text{blue}\, p_\text{blue}
$$

For a green target, $g = (0, 1, 0)$, so $p_\text{target} = 0 \cdot p_\text{red} + 1 \cdot p_\text{green} + 0 \cdot p_\text{blue} = p_\text{green}$. The MLP then only sees **one** target position, together with the end-effector, the gripper and the bin, and the colors never reach it. The network is the same MLP action-chunking policy with MSE loss as before.

This has a nice side effect: "red target at $y = 0.35$" and "green target at $y = 0.35$" become exactly the same input, so every demonstration trains all three colors at once. I only recorded red-target demos. To check that this really works, I compared colors within the same layout on the first model. For example, with the target in slot 1 and the bin in slot 2, red succeeds 19/20, green 13/15 and blue 10/10.

It also connects back to the loss discussion above. Selecting the target before the network removes one source of multimodality: for a given input there is again essentially one correct action, which is exactly the condition under which MSE works.

### Covering Every Layout

The color problem is gone, but the distribution is still more complex than in the earlier exercises, because the route depends on where the target and the bin are. To get a high success rate from limited data, every one of the 12 combinations needs to show up in the demonstrations. A random layout per episode would cover some combinations several times and miss others, so I changed the recorder to step through the 12 combinations in order and only move on after an episode is saved. I recorded three rounds, so **36 demos**, exactly 3 per combination.

### Why the Policy Never Lets Go

About 90% of the failures look the same. The cube is picked up and carried to within 1 to 2 cm of the bin center, and then the arm just **hovers** there until the 800-step limit. The gripper never opens.

The grasping and carrying part works, so the problem is the release. The reason turned out to be in my own demonstrations: **pauses during teleoperation**. When I teleoperate with the keyboard, there are many short moments where I am not pressing anything. Each of those moments is recorded as a step where the arm does not move and the gripper command does not change. Across all demos, **53%** of the recorded steps are idle like this.

  width={720}

/>

The left side shows one demo: long shaded stretches where nothing happens, including most of the time the cube is over the bin. The right side counts this over all 36 demos. While holding the cube over the bin, **739 of 1048 steps (71%)** are idle. So in exactly the state where the policy should release, the label it sees most often is "stay still, keep holding". The "open" label shows up for only a few steps.

This would not be a problem if the policy knew how long it had been waiting, but the state only contains positions. Two states, "just arrived over the bin" and "have been waiting here for a while", look identical. The policy is trained to predict the mean of the labels, and in that state the mean is dominated by "stay still". So it learns to hover. In the MSE section I said that averaging out teleop noise can help. Here the pauses are not random noise around a motion; they are a systematic label that outweighs the one action that matters.

The fix needs no new data: drop the idle transitions before training. Steps where the gripper is opening or closing are kept, and the arm path does not change, because idle steps have zero motion anyway. The dataset shrinks from 10186 to 4757 transitions, and over the bin only the 309 steps that actually do something remain.

## Closing Thoughts

The main lessons from this homework were about data rather than the network. A small MLP was enough in all three exercises, but only after the data covered the states the policy actually visits. DAgger fixed the center-zone failures in Exercise 1. In Exercise 2, collecting targeted data for the under-represented minus and plus zones turned an OOD failure (0/83 outside the center zone) into a policy that succeeds in most episodes in every zone. I also learned to choose models by closed-loop success rate instead of validation loss, and not to trust a single seed.

Exercise 3 added two more lessons. First, break the problem down before collecting data: listing the layouts showed there are only 12 of them, and noticing that the colors do not matter without vision let me remove the goal-reading problem from the network entirely, so that every demo counts for all three colors. Second, look at *how* the policy fails, not just how often. The hovering failure looked like a model problem, but it came from my own pauses during teleoperation, and removing them raised the mean success rate from 50% to 65% without recording a single new demo.

The MSE loss carried through all three exercises, because in each of them I could arrange things so that a state has essentially one correct action: the obstacle position separates the zones, and the target selection removes the colors. That will not always be possible. When the demonstrations contain several valid ways to solve the same state, MSE averages between them. The natural next step is **flow matching**, which learns the full distribution of action chunks. Instead of regressing one action, it learns a velocity field that transforms Gaussian noise into an action chunk step by step, so at test time the policy *samples* one valid mode instead of averaging them. I want to try it on this multi-cube task and compare it with the MSE policy in a future post.
