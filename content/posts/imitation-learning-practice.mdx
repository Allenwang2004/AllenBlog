This homework walks through the main parts of a modern imitation learning pipeline. I teleoperate a simulated **SO-101** arm to record expert demonstrations, train a policy to imitate the expert actions, and then push the success rate up during evaluation. The task is to pick up a red cube, move it around a wall-shaped obstacle, and drop it into a bin.

The post has three parts: how the data is collected and what the policy looks like, the two exercises (plain behavior cloning, then DAgger), and a closer look at the loss function. I train with a plain **MSE loss**, so the policy predicts the *mean* action, not a *distribution* over actions. That choice explains both why this setup works here and where it will stop working.

## Collecting Expert Data

The first step is recording expert demonstrations by teleoperating the arm with the keyboard. At first this took me a lot of time, because the default camera angle was pretty weird and it was hard to judge depth. After I changed the camera angle, teleoperating the arm became much easier.

Each recorded step stores the simulator state. The actions are then computed as differences between consecutive states, $a_t = s_{t+1} - s_t$. I use end-effector control, so an action is the change of the end-effector position plus a gripper command.

## Policy Architecture

The policy is a simple MLP action-chunking policy:

- **Input (12-dim state):** end-effector position (3), gripper angle (1), cube position plus the first two quaternion components (5), and obstacle position (3). Every dimension is normalized with the mean and std of the training data.
- **Network:** fully connected layers of width 256 with ReLU (5 hidden layers for Exercise 1, 6 for Exercise 2), followed by a linear output layer.
- **Output:** an action chunk of the next 16 steps. Each step is 4-dim: the change of the end-effector position $(dx, dy, dz)$ and the gripper command. At evaluation time the robot executes the whole chunk before predicting the next one.
- **Loss:** MSE between the predicted and the recorded action chunk.
- **Training:** AdamW (lr $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine learning-rate schedule, 200 epochs, batch size 256. For every configuration I train 5 models with seeds 0 to 4.

Predicting a **chunk** of 16 actions instead of a single action is a cheap but effective trick. The policy commits to a short motion segment, so small per-step errors have fewer chances to pile up, and the motion is smoother than re-deciding at every step.

### Why Do the Seeds Differ So Much?

The spread between seeds is huge, from 36% to 88% with the same data and the same hyperparameters. At first I thought the main reason was the train/validation split: the seed decides which samples go into the validation set, and with DAgger data in the mix it matters a lot whether the few DAgger corrections end up in training or get "wasted" in validation.

To be more precise, the seed changes three things at once: the initial weights, the order of the mini-batches, and which 10% of the samples go to the validation set. The split is not the only source of the variance. Even when I trained on the teleop data only, three seeds gave 61%, 73% and 67%. Two changes came out of this:

- **Validation uses teleop data only.** When DAgger data is present, the validation set is sampled only from the original teleop episodes, and all DAgger samples are always used for training. This way no DAgger correction is wasted in the validation set.
- **Model selection by success rate, not by validation loss.** The validation loss does not track the success rate in simulation, so the best-validation checkpoint of a seed is not necessarily a good policy. Instead I evaluate all 5 seeds with the official evaluation (100 episodes, seed 42) and submit the one with the highest success rate.

The second point deserves an explanation. The validation loss measures how close the predicted actions are to the expert's actions, **on states taken from the expert's own trajectories**. The success rate measures what happens when the policy runs **closed-loop**: every small error moves the robot into a slightly different state, the next prediction is made from that state, and errors compound. This mismatch between the training distribution and the states the policy visits is called covariate shift, and it is exactly the problem DAgger is designed to address. A low open-loop MSE says little about whether the policy recovers from its own mistakes.

## A Closer Look at the Loss: MSE Predicts the Mean

One thing I want to highlight is that this whole homework uses only an **MSE loss**:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s, a) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \left\| a - f_\theta(s) \right\|^2 \right]
$$

For a fixed state $s$, the prediction that minimizes this loss is the **conditional mean** of the expert's actions:

$$
f^*(s) = \mathbb{E}[\, a \mid s \,]
$$

You can see this by expanding the loss for a single state: $\mathbb{E}\|a - c\|^2 = \|c - \mathbb{E}[a]\|^2 + \mathrm{Var}(a)$, and the variance term does not depend on the prediction $c$, so the best choice is $c = \mathbb{E}[a]$. Equivalently, MSE is maximum likelihood under a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(f_\theta(s), \sigma^2 I)$ with fixed variance. So an MSE policy implicitly assumes that, for every state, the expert's actions form **one unimodal blob**, and it outputs the center of that blob. It does not learn the **distribution** of actions, so it cannot represent the fact that there may be several equally good ways to act.

### Why That Is a Problem in General

Imagine the expert reaches a wall and goes around it on the left in half of the demos and on the right in the other half. The conditional mean of "go left" and "go right" is "go straight", which drives the arm right into the wall. The averaged action is not just suboptimal; it may not be a valid action at all. This is the **mode averaging** problem, and it is the main weakness of MSE behavior cloning whenever the demonstrations are **multimodal**.

### Why MSE Works in This Task

In this homework, MSE was good enough to reach full points, for a few reasons:

- **The obstacle position is in the input.** In each zone there is basically one correct route, and the network can tell the zones apart from the obstacle position. The multimodality across zones (left detour in the center zone, straight path in the minus zone) is resolved by conditioning, so for a given state the expert's action is close to unimodal.
- **A single, consistent expert.** All demos come from me, with one strategy per zone. The plot of the training data shows how tightly the center-zone teleop demos follow the same detour.
- **Action chunking.** Predicting 16 steps at once makes the policy commit to a whole motion segment, so it is less likely to flip between strategies from one step to the next.
- **Averaging can even help.** Human teleoperation is noisy (small overshoots, pauses, jitter). Predicting the mean smooths that noise out, which is a useful side effect when the noise is just noise and not a different strategy.

Even here there are hints of the limit. In the plus zone, some of my DAgger demos take a wide loop to the left while others go almost straight up. When the data contains two styles like this from similar states, an MSE policy can only output something in between them. That is fine as long as "in between" is still collision-free, but there is no guarantee that it is.

## Closing Thoughts

The main lessons from this homework were about data rather than the network. A small MLP was enough, but only after the data covered the states the policy actually visits. DAgger fixed the center-zone failures in Exercise 1. In Exercise 2, collecting targeted data for the under-represented minus and plus zones turned an OOD failure (0/83 outside the center zone) into a policy that succeeds in most episodes in every zone. I also learned to choose models by closed-loop success rate instead of validation loss, and not to trust a single seed.

The MSE loss is the part that will not carry over. It works here because each state has essentially one correct action. The next exercise is a **multi-goal** problem: three cubes and a randomized bin, with the policy told which cube to pick. There, the demonstrations become much more multimodal, and averaging between modes becomes a real risk. For that problem I will switch from MSE to **flow matching**, which learns the full distribution of action chunks. Instead of regressing one action, it learns a velocity field that transforms Gaussian noise into an action chunk step by step, so at test time the policy *samples* one valid mode instead of averaging them.
